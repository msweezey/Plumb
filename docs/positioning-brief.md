# Plumb positioning brief

> **Status: draft.** This brief is written from the conclusion of the Oct 12 strategy session: Plumb is not an agency. It's an MSP moving up the stack, replacing internal engineering and SaaS procurement, and retainers are the goal. The original notes in the glitch repo haven't been reviewed yet. Anything marked *(to validate)* is a proposal, not a decision.

## The one-liner

**Plumb builds and runs the software your business depends on, for a monthly fee.**
It's a managed service provider that has moved up the stack. Other MSPs keep the laptops and the network running. Plumb keeps the business's own tools running: the internal apps, workflows and integrations that are currently held together by SaaS seats, spreadsheets and the one person who knows how it works.

## Who buys

- **Company:** Operations-heavy businesses that are too big for duct tape but too small to justify an engineering team. They run on a stack of SaaS tools plus spreadsheets and automations. *(to validate: likely 20 to 500 employees.)*
- **Buyer:** The person who owns operating costs and vendor contracts. That's usually a COO, CFO, head of operations, or the founder or GM. It's the same person who already signs the IT/MSP contract, not a marketing leader.
- **Triggers:**
  - A big SaaS renewal is coming up.
  - The in-house developer or "the person who built the Airtable" leaves.
  - A process outgrows its no-code tools.
  - A tool almost fits but needs three workarounds.
  - Leadership asks why software spend keeps going up.

## What Plumb replaces

| Today they pay for… | The pain | With Plumb |
|---|---|---|
| **Internal engineering:** a developer or two, contractors, or an ops person who codes on the side | Expensive, hard to hire, hard to manage, and a single point of failure | One retainer that covers building, maintenance and on-call, with no hiring risk |
| **SaaS procurement:** point tools, per-seat licenses, integration glue (Zapier and similar) | Seat sprawl, overlapping tools, workflows bent to fit the software | Fewer subscriptions, plus custom tools built around how the business actually works |
| **Agency or freelance projects** | Built, handed off, then left to rot. Nobody owns it after launch | Plumb owns it after launch, and that ongoing ownership is the product |

The pitch isn't "we'll build you an app." It's "**stop buying and babysitting software, and let us run it.**"

## The offer: a retainer, not a project

What a Plumb retainer includes:

1. **Onboarding and audit:** map the current tools, spend and workflows, and agree a roadmap of what to build, consolidate or cut.
2. **Build:** custom internal tools, integrations and automations, shipped in small increments.
3. **Run:** hosting, monitoring, security updates, backups and fixes, with an agreed support response time.
4. **Improve:** a set amount of build capacity every month for new requests, so the system keeps up with the business.
5. **Report:** a monthly summary of what shipped, uptime, and SaaS spend removed.

Rules that protect the model:
- **No standalone projects.** Any build work goes onto a retainer.
- **The client owns their code and data.** Lock-in comes from the service being good, not from contracts or hostage code. This also makes the buying decision easier.
- **Capacity, not hours.** Clients buy a level of ongoing service, not a timesheet.

## Pricing shape *(to validate)*

- **Onboarding fee:** a fixed price for the audit, migration and first build, so the first month isn't run at a loss.
- **Monthly retainer, in tiers:** each tier is set by the number of systems Plumb manages and the build capacity per month. Example tiers: *Run* (maintain what exists), *Run + Build* (maintenance plus regular new work), and *Embedded* (a dedicated squad).
- **Anchor against what it replaces, not against agency rates.** The comparison is the fully loaded cost of an internal engineer plus the SaaS line items that get cancelled. The retainer should come in well below that combined number while still keeping healthy margins.
- **Annual terms with a discount for prepayment.** Recurring revenue is the point of the business.

## Why "MSP" beats "agency"

| | Agency | MSP (Plumb) |
|---|---|---|
| **Unit of sale** | A project with an end date | An ongoing service with no end date |
| **Revenue** | Lumpy, so the agency is always pitching for the next project | Recurring and predictable, which also makes the business worth more |
| **Budget line** | Marketing or one-off capital spend, which gets cut first | Operations or IT, which the business can't switch off |
| **What success looks like** | Launch day | It keeps working and keeps getting better |
| **Relationship after delivery** | Handoff, then churn | Deeper over time as more systems come under management |
| **Comparison the buyer makes** | Against other agencies and freelancers, on day rates | Against hiring engineers and renewing SaaS, on total cost |
| **Familiarity** | "Agency" signals creative and marketing work | Buyers already know and trust the MSP model from IT |

"Agency" makes the retainer something Plumb has to fight for. "MSP" makes it the default: buyers already expect to pay an MSP monthly for something that just works. Calling Plumb an MSP also points the sales conversation at the right person (ops and finance) and the right comparison (headcount and SaaS spend).

## Messaging guardrails

- **Say:** managed, run, own, operate, retainer, uptime, consolidate, "your software team."
- **Avoid:** agency, project, campaign, deliverable, "we build apps."
- **Proof to collect:** SaaS spend removed, hours saved, and systems under management per client.
